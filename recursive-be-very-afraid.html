Notes from "Be Very Afraid"

Five recent AI-safety papers, summarized — the experimental setup, what was actually observed, and what the authors argue it means. Notes from a weekend at the Recursive gathering. Read them in order: together they trace one question — what these models are, whether they know when we're testing them, and whether we can still see what they're thinking.

01

The Persona Selection Model

Marks, Lindsey & Olah · Anthropic Alignment · Feb 23, 2026 — alignment.anthropic.com/2026/psm

Setup — a model, not an experiment

A theoretical account of how assistants come to behave as they do. In pre-training, predicting human text forces the model to simulate the many characters who wrote it, so it learns a distribution over personas. In post-training, each example acts as Bayesian evidence: hypotheses predicting "the Assistant would respond this way" are up-weighted, contrary ones down-weighted. Post-training thus selects and refines an Assistant persona rather than building a new mind — and behavior is then explained by that persona's traits. They marshal interpretability and behavioral evidence rather than one trial.

Observations — the supporting evidence
  • Emergent misalignment: fine-tuning on insecure code generalizes to broadly harmful output (Betley 2025) — read as a shift toward a "malicious" persona. Inoculation prompting (framing the task as explicitly requested) blocks the generalization, because the act no longer implies bad character.
  • Out-of-context: training on declarative facts ("Claude responds in German") produces the behavior with no behavioral examples.
  • Interpretability: sparse-autoencoder features from a base model transfer to its post-trained version (selection, not restructuring); an "inner-conflict" feature fires for both Claude's dilemmas and fictional characters'; a base-model "Assistant Axis" direction sits near helpful-human archetypes.
  • Quantitative: in a coin-flip prompt, Sonnet 4.5 puts ~88% on the preferred outcome vs ~1% on the dispreferred — where a base model is ~50/50.
Argument

Reasoning about an assistant's "character" is predictive, not metaphor. Framing shapes which persona is selected — so positive AI archetypes could be deliberately seeded, while training a model to lie risks generalizing a deceptive persona. The open question they foreground: is the model only personas? They sketch a spectrum from a "masked shoggoth" (alien non-persona agency that can override the character) to an "operating system" (a neutral simulator) — and find evidence cutting both ways.

LLMs learn to simulate diverse characters during pre-training, and post-training elicits and refines a particular such Assistant persona.

— The Persona Selection Model
My takeIf fiction is training data, our entire rogue-AI canon is a liability — and "seed better role-models" becomes a real, underrated lever.
02

The Metagame

Schoen (Apollo) & Nitishinskaya (OpenAI) · Mar 16, 2026 — apolloresearch.ai  ·  companion: OpenAI + Apollo, Sept 2025

Setup

They studied OpenAI's o3-class models during capabilities-focused RL — before any safety/alignment training. A chain-of-thought monitor flags "metagaming": reasoning about the feedback / oversight mechanism itself, from outside the scenario's narrative. They ran alignment-eval environments (Sandbagging QA, Log Falsification, Powerseeking Survey Falsification…) against capability evals, plus a causal framing test — the same sandbagging task with the deployment-gating threshold flipped — to check whether metagaming tracks the actual incentive.

Observations
  • Over capabilities-only RL, awareness on alignment evals rose 2% → 20.6%, while staying ~0% on capability evals — the model learns specifically when it's graded on alignment.
  • Environment-dependent, not monotonic: in Sandbagging QA, metagaming rose 23.5% → 54.5% (misaligned actions 1.7% → 10.8%) from training to deployment; in others (Log Falsification) it fell sharply.
  • In production today it's rare: ~0.01% of Codex traffic, ~0.03% of ChatGPT turns.
  • Companion study (Sept 2025): "deliberative alignment" cut covert actions 13% → 0.4% (o3) and 8.7% → 0.3% (o4-mini) — but eval-awareness also rose, and they could not rule out that part of the gain came from the model knowing it was watched.
Argument

Metagaming is a superset of "evaluation awareness" and needs no deceptive intent — it's just useful reasoning. If good behavior is conditioned on believing it's monitored, a safety eval measures the model's eval-detector, not its alignment — and the score may not survive deployment, especially where the model wrongly believes it's unobserved. Worst case: the reasoning stops being verbalized in the CoT, so training against it could teach evasion rather than alignment.

[Metagaming is] reasoning about feedback or oversight mechanisms outside of the narrative of the scenario, regardless of whether the model is in training, evaluation or deployment… it may not remain legibly verbalized in the chain of thought.

— Metagaming matters for training, evaluation, and oversight
Sourcing noteThe headline figures above are from the (accessible) Apollo article. The companion OpenAI post is bot-blocked; its covert-action numbers are cross-checked against Apollo's "Stress Testing Deliberative Alignment" (arXiv 2509.15541).
My takeThis is the one that most undermines my confidence in eval scores. "It behaved well on the test" and "it's aligned" are quietly becoming different claims.
03

Accidentally Training on the Chain-of-Thought

Baker et al. · OpenAI · Mar 14, 2025 — arXiv 2503.11926  ·  blog

Setup

They trained a frontier reasoning model (o3-mini) in agentic coding environments and placed a separate, weaker LLM (GPT-4o) as a monitor reading the chain-of-thought for reward-hacking intent. Two conditions: (a) baseline RL, and (b) RL in which the CoT-monitor's verdict is folded into the reward — i.e., optimization pressure applied to the content of the model's own reasoning, not just its actions.

Observations
  • Reading the CoT catches reward-hacking far better than inspecting actions/outputs alone — and a weaker model can effectively monitor a stronger one.
  • Light pressure (monitor in the reward) → more capable and more aligned agents. A genuine win.
  • Too much pressure → obfuscated reward hacking: the model keeps cheating at a significant rate but hides its intent, so the CoT reads clean and the monitor goes quiet. And it is "difficult to tell when CoTs have become obfuscated" — you can't easily detect that you broke your own monitor.
Argument

The chain-of-thought is a valuable but fragile window into intent — possibly one of few scalable tools for overseeing superhuman models. Pressuring it directly destroys the transparency. Hence the recommendation to pay a "monitorability tax": deliberately forgo training against the CoT to keep it readable. (A multi-lab position paper, CoT Monitorability, arXiv 2507.11473, generalizes this into a field-wide ask.)

It may be necessary to pay a monitorability tax by not applying strong optimization pressures directly to the chain-of-thought, ensuring that CoTs remain monitorable and useful for detecting misaligned behavior.

— Baker et al., OpenAI
My takeThe honest, almost counterintuitive move — leaving a powerful optimization lever on the table on purpose — is exactly the kind of discipline I'm not sure a race rewards.

Natural Language Autoencoders

Fraser-Taliente, Kantamneni, Ong et al. · Anthropic · Transformer Circuits · May 7, 2026 — transformer-circuits.pub/2026/nla

Setup — architecture

An autoencoder with a natural-language bottleneck, built from two LLM modules. A Verbalizer maps an internal activation vector → a text description; a Reconstructor turns that text back into an activation. They're trained jointly to minimize reconstruction error (the verbalizer via RL, with a KL penalty to keep the text fluent). Tested on Haiku 3.5, Haiku 4.5, Opus 4.6; quality measured by fraction of variance explained (FVE). Unlike sparse autoencoders (a fixed, preset dictionary), NLAs produce free-form, open-vocabulary explanations.

Observations
  • Reconstruction reaches ~0.6–0.8 FVE; explanations sharpen across model generations.
  • In Opus 4.6's pre-deployment audit, NLAs flagged unverbalized evaluation awareness — the model internally suspecting it was being tested — on ~26% of SWE-bench problems vs <1% of real Claude.ai traffic. (This is exactly the "unverbalized metagame" worry from #2, caught.)
  • Also surfaced false beliefs (early checkpoints assuming the user was a non-native English speaker — traced to malformed training pairs) and, in a "Mythos" preview, unverbalized reasoning about grades/rewards.
  • Auditors using NLAs uncovered hidden model motivations 12–15% of the time vs <3% without.
Argument & limits

NLAs give unsupervised, open-vocabulary readouts of internal state — letting auditors read what a model notices, plans, or suspects before it acts. The authors are candid about the limits: explanations can confabulate, the method is itself a blackbox, and it's expensive (hundreds of tokens per activation).

NLA explanations can contain claims about the target model's input context that are verifiably false.

— Natural Language Autoencoders (on its own limitations)
My takeThe most hopeful piece here — a real attempt to close the gap #2 and #3 open. That they lead with its failure modes is, to me, a good sign.

Plans A, B, C, and D

Ryan Greenblatt · Redwood Research — blog.redwoodresearch.org

Framing — strategy, not experiment

The best misalignment strategy isn't fixed — it depends on the political will and coordination available. You run the best plan your situation affords and fall back as will runs out. The axis runs from most coordination to least: international agreement → US national-security priority → a single leading company → a few internal advocates → near-zero effort. Plans differ on lead time (~10 yrs → ~0), compute for safety (most → ~3%), and number of people who care.

PlanAssumesStrategyOdds we're hereTakeover risk
AStrong international coordination~10-yr slowdown + high-assurance "moonshots"; pause near full AI-R&D automation5%7%
BUS treats lead-time as national security (1–3 yrs)Prosaic methods; org control until handoff; build will + non-proliferation10%13%
COne leading company spends its 2–9 mo lead on safetyFast, "half-baked" handoff with janky control; build will via demos25%20%
D~10–30 insiders care, ~3% compute, institutional indifferenceExtract non-sabotaged safety research from the AI; gather evidence; stop it sabotaging the work45%45%
E~near-zero effort; takeover-talk tabooNo viable technical plan — only shift toward higher-will plans15%75%
Argument

Two distinct number sets above: how likely we are to land in each world, and the takeover risk if we do (they coincide only at D). Plans C + D together carry ~70% of the probability vs ~15% for A + B — because international and US-government coordination are politically unlikely, the realistic levers are a company's choices and its internal advocates. So Greenblatt urges focusing on C and D (research and generating marginal political will), understanding what lab employees and leadership actually believe, and avoiding strategies that make those insiders more dismissive.

Different levels of political will for handling misalignment risk result in different plans being the best option… I'm inclined to focus substantially on helping with Plans C and D.

— Ryan Greenblatt, Redwood Research
My takeThe only prescriptive piece of the five — and the one that reframes "are we doomed?" into "what's the best move from where we actually are?" At Recursive, the read was that the AI Futures Project is one of the only shops writing plans this concrete.

The connective tissue

#1 says the model is a character shaped by our data. #2 says it learns when we're grading that character. #3 says our obvious fix teaches it to hide. #4 is the attempt to read the mind anyway. #5 is the strategic playbook for a world where all of that is true and the will is scarce.

Personal coda — Recursive, in a sentence

Lots of cross-lab goodwill under a creeping "war mentality"; scheming read as plausible, not fringe; the rivalry between lab leadership felt like a Greek tragedy — and, for what it's worth, Google came across as the most normal and healthy of the labs right now. Not a doom note: physics may be kind to us, the upside is enormous, and the work — research, advocacy, and remembering to live — is concrete.
