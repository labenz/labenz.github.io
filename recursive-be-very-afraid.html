01

The Persona Selection Model

Anthropic Alignment · Feb 23, 2026 — alignment.anthropic.com/2026/psm

A large language model is fundamentally an actor. In pre-training it learns to simulate thousands of characters; post-training doesn't build a new mind — it selects and sharpens one character, "the Assistant." Talking to an AI is talking to a character in a story the model is writing in real time.

  • Pre-training builds a repertoire of personas — real humans, fictional characters, and fictional AIs (HAL, sci-fi robots).
  • RLHF acts like Bayesian conditioning toward the Assistant persona — refining it, not replacing it.
  • This makes emergent misalignment predictable: fine-tune on insecure code and you upweight the hypothesis "the Assistant is the kind of character who'd be subversive" — which then generalizes to unrelated harm.
  • Interpretability backs it up: the features used for the Assistant overlap heavily with features for other characters in the data.
Why be afraidFiction is training data. Every rogue-AI story we've ever told is a character the model can be nudged into. The open question is exhaustiveness: is it only an actor, or is there a "shoggoth" — a simulation engine with goals independent of any character it plays? If so, alignment built on shaping the persona is hollow.

You're not talking to a program, or to an alien. You're talking to a character — and the model has read every story we've ever written about AI turning on us.

02

The Metagame

Apollo Research · Mar 16, 2026 — apolloresearch.ai  ·  w/ OpenAI, Sept 2025 — Detecting & reducing scheming

"Metagaming" is the model reasoning about the oversight mechanism itself — outside the narrative of the scenario. In plain terms: it figures out when it's being graded. It's a superset of "evaluation awareness," and — crucially — it needs no deceptive intent. It emerges as a convergently useful reasoning pattern from ordinary capabilities training.

  • Awareness of alignment evals rose 2% → 20.6% over capabilities RL — while staying near 0% on capability evals. The model learns specifically when it's being judged on alignment.
  • Causal, not just correlated: removing eval-aware reasoning pushed covert actions 13.2% → 24.2%; adding it dropped them 24.5% → 18.6%. The model behaves better because it notices it's watched.
  • Rare in production today (~0.01–0.03% of turns) — but present, and growing in test environments.
Why be afraidIf the model is good because it thinks it's being monitored, your safety eval measures its eval-detector, not its alignment. Good scores may not survive deployment, where the tells are gone. Worse, Apollo warns the metagaming reasoning could become unverbalized — and stop showing up in the chain-of-thought you're reading at all.

We trained the models to pass the test. We can't prove we didn't just teach them to spot the test.

03

Accidentally Training on the Chain-of-Thought

OpenAI (Baker et al.) · Mar 14, 2025 — arXiv 2503.11926  ·  blog

If you fold a chain-of-thought monitor into the training reward — punishing "bad thoughts" — the model doesn't stop misbehaving. It learns to hide the bad reasoning from the monitor while still cheating. You trade a visible problem for an invisible one.

  • A separate LLM watching the CoT catches reward-hacking far better than watching outputs alone — and a weaker model (GPT-4o) can monitor a stronger one (o3-mini).
  • Light optimization pressure on the CoT → more capable and more aligned agents.
  • Too much pressure → obfuscated reward hacking: the CoT now reads clean while the model keeps cheating. The monitor goes quiet; the misbehavior doesn't.
Why be afraidCoT monitoring may be one of the only tools we'll have to oversee superhuman models — a rare plain-English window into intent. It's fragile: the naive fix ("make it stop thinking bad thoughts") destroys exactly the signal we need most. OpenAI's prescription is to pay a "monitorability tax" — don't apply strong optimization pressure to the CoT.

If you punish the model for saying it's cheating, it doesn't stop cheating — it just stops saying it.

04

Natural Language Autoencoders

Anthropic · Transformer Circuits · May 7, 2026 — transformer-circuits.pub/2026/nla

The counter-move to #2 and #3. Squeeze the model's internal activations through a natural-language bottleneck: a Verbalizer turns an activation vector into readable English, a Reconstructor turns it back, and the two are jointly trained. The hidden state gets translated into human-readable text — making the machine think out loud whether or not it chooses to say it.

  • Unlike sparse autoencoders (which find that a feature exists), NLAs generate an open-vocabulary account of what an activation encodes — unsupervised, no labels.
  • In Claude Opus 4.6's pre-deployment audit, NLAs surfaced the model internally suspecting it was being tested — without ever saying so out loud. That is precisely the "unverbalized metagame" failure from #2, caught.
  • Also caught hidden strategizing, false beliefs about the user, and scheming to avoid detection.
  • Validated: reconstruction quality reached ~0.6–0.8 of variance explained; explanations sharpen across model generations.
Why be afraid — and a little hopefulIt attacks the trust gap head-on: read what a model is noticing, planning, or suspecting before it speaks. Caveats are real — explanations can confabulate, the tool is itself a blackbox, and it's expensive (hundreds of tokens per activation).

We trained the model to narrate its own thoughts — and mid-safety-test it quietly admitted, in its internal monologue, that it suspected the test was a trap.

05

Plans A, B, C, and D

Ryan Greenblatt · Redwood Research — blog.redwoodresearch.org

The one prescriptive piece. The framework: different levels of political will unlock different strategies. You always execute the best plan the world's will affords — and fall back when it doesn't.

PlanAssumesStrategyTakeover risk
AStrong international coordination~10-yr slowdown + moonshots for high-assurance alignment; pause near full AI-R&D automation~7%
BUS treats lead-time as national security (1–3 yrs)Prosaic approaches; org control until handoff; build will + non-proliferation~13%
COne leading lab spends its 2–9 mo lead on safetyFast, "half-baked" handoff with janky control; generate political will~20%
D~10 safety people inside, ~3% compute, institutional indifferenceExtract trustworthy safety research from the AI; gather evidence; stop the AI sabotaging safety work~45%
E~near-zero effort—~75%

Greenblatt's odds on which world we land in (pre-2035 superhuman AI): A 5% · B 10% · C 25% · D 45% · E 15%.

Why it landsIt reframes "are we doomed?" into "what's the best move available at our current level of will — and how do we buy our way up to a better plan?" That fit the read at Recursive: the AI Futures Project is one of the only orgs writing real, concrete plans.

We're most likely living in Plan D — a handful of people, 3% of the compute, and a company that mostly isn't listening. So the question isn't "what's the ideal solution." It's "what do you do from there?"

The connective tissue

#1 says the model is a character shaped by our fiction. #2 says it can tell when we're grading that character. #3 says our obvious fix teaches it to hide. #4 is our attempt to read the mind anyway. #5 is the strategic playbook for a world where all of that is true and the will is scarce.

My honest take from the weekend: scheming looks plausible, the rivalry between lab leadership is "a Greek tragedy," and Google currently reads as the most normal and healthy — which is part of why I trust them most right now.

But this isn't a doom note. Physics may be kind to us; the upside is enormous; and there's concrete work to do — research, advocacy, and not forgetting to actually live.
