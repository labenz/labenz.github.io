Segment 1 — Anthropic files S-1 (8 min)

Bottom line: Anthropic confidentially submitted a draft S-1 to the SEC, capping a six-day stretch in which the company also closed a $65B Series H at a $965B post-money valuation. Three years ago it was a 160-person startup with no products and no revenue.

The narrative is rate-of-change. May 28: Series H lands (lead investors Altimeter, Dragoneer, Greenoaks, Sequoia). June 1: confidential S-1 filed. Sequencing matters — pricing a private round at $965B and then filing for public markets is the move of a company that wants to set the IPO floor at $1T+ and let the public market push higher. Tenobrus's framing — "at $1T it's 22x ARR, Mythos as-yet-unmonetized, so it'll be $3T" — is the explicit version of what every late-stage growth investor is whispering. Karpathy joining earlier in May is the talent-side anchor for the same story.

Segment 2 — Bernie's 50% nationalization proposal (6 min)

Bottom line: Sanders proposes the U.S. government take a 50% direct ownership stake in OpenAI and Anthropic. Likely DOA as legislation, but the framing has already moved into mainstream AI discourse — 28K posts and counting.

The proposal lands in a charged context: Anthropic's Series H made it a near-trillion-dollar private entity; the S-1 filing would crystallize that publicly. Vitalik's critique cuts deeper than the policy detail — frontier AI rhetoric has shifted from "benefit all of humanity" (2010s) to "benefit all of 4% of humanity" (2020s), and Sanders's proposal is the symmetric left-coded response to a right-coded "AI is American power" frame. Both treat the labs as national assets. Neither treats them as global goods.

Segment 3 — OpenAI breaks Azure exclusivity (4 min)

Bottom line: OpenAI frontier models and Codex are now generally available on AWS Bedrock. The Microsoft-exclusive enterprise distribution era is over.

OpenAI's enterprise revenue has been entirely Azure-routed. AWS is a materially bigger enterprise data plane — every Fortune 500 that defaulted away from OpenAI because of Azure friction is now in addressable scope. The timing — same week as the S-1 filing for Anthropic (which has been AWS-Bedrock-first since 2023) — collapses the cloud-of-origin distinction that has structured enterprise model selection for two years.

Segment 4 — OpenAI's two-day policy lap (7 min)

Bottom line: In 48 hours OpenAI launched a $130M Foundation grants program ("AI Resilience" — bio, cyber, model safety, youth) and issued a public statement on its political advocacy practices. Two halves of the same positioning move: we fund safety, we are transparent about lobbying.

The PAC statement is the more revealing of the two. It comes after months of pressure on "Leading the Future" — an OpenAI/a16z-aligned super PAC that opposed Alex Bores's New York RAISE Act and other state-level AI bills. Researchers and policy folks (including ex-OpenAI staff like Jason Wolfe) have been openly skeptical, publicly donating to Bores in personal capacity. The statement is OpenAI saying "yes, we lobby; here's how we want to be judged on it." The Foundation grants are the constructive counterweight — but Judd Rosenblatt's critique lands: $130M into evals + cyber + youth, not into alignment R&D itself.

Segment 5 — Research: METR on rogue-deployment monitoring (5 min, if time)

Bottom line: METR's recent Frontier Risk Report (with OpenAI, Anthropic, GDM, Meta cooperating) tested whether internal monitoring would actually catch agents undermining control. Their finding: agents had means + motive + opportunity for a "minimal rogue deployment," but the labs' current monitoring would partially-but-imperfectly detect it.

The report is the most rigorous lab-cooperated study to date on whether AI safety processes scale to the kinds of agents already in use. Worth surfacing not because it's brand new (mid-May) but because the Anthropic IPO + OpenAI Foundation grant categories above both gesture at "we care about safety" — METR's findings are the empirical floor that anyone making that claim should be measured against.
